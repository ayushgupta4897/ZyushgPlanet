ZyushgPlanet · Kepler Bonus archival search · Report, 17 October 2026

Three sub-Neptune planet candidates

Each has a false-positive probability below 1 % once Gaia's limits on unseen companion stars are applied. They orbit faint stars that the Kepler telescope recorded from 2009 to 2013 without ever targeting them, and none appears in any catalogue we checked.

Pixel localisation

Where the light goes missing

Each candidate sits in the pixel stamp of a brighter star that Kepler was observing. For every transit we subtract the in-transit image from the images just before and after. We then stack the quarters of each Kepler season, when the star falls on the same detector, and ask which star the missing light belongs to.

False positives

Below the 1.5 % line

TRICERATOPS weighs the planet hypothesis against eclipsing binaries and against planets around other stars, on the target and on every neighbour within ten Kepler pixels. With default priors the three come out at 2.4, 2.7 and 3.6 %. Almost all of that is one scenario: a planet around a fainter, unseen companion star. Gaia constrains those companions twice, through its astrometry and through its imaging. MOLUSC turns both into a companion population, and what remains is too faint to make these dips.

Consistency

The transit shape fits the star

How long a transit lasts, and how sharply it begins and ends, fixes the density of the star being crossed. A planet around an unseen companion would usually imply the wrong density. For all three candidates, the density from the transit matches Gaia's measurement of the host to within half a standard deviation.

The search

From 6,465 stars to three

Each dot is one star from the pre-registered target list: background stars on the Kepler pixels that Kepler never targeted and that no published transit search had covered. Thresholds were frozen before the search, the same search ran on every light curve turned upside down, and positive controls had to pass before any result was read. The counts follow the path the three candidates took; two other signals survived review but have inconclusive pixel tests.

    Status

    What each candidate has passed

    A planet candidate is a signal that survives every test the data allow. Validation needs one more step, a refereed paper, and in practice a high-resolution image of each host to replace Gaia's statistical companion limits inside half an arcsecond.

    What this report claims

    • Three transit signals that are periodic, localised on the target star in Kepler's own pixels, and absent from the inverted-flux null.
    • Each has a false-positive probability between 0.6 and 0.7 % with Gaia constraints, and 0 % from nearby resolved stars.
    • None matches the period and timing of any known Kepler object of interest, threshold-crossing event or eclipsing binary anywhere in the Kepler field.

    What it does not claim

    • These are not confirmed or validated planets. No mass has been measured and no paper has been refereed.
    • The stage-2 vetting rules and both pixel rules were set after early results had been seen. Every such change is dated in the pre-registration log and labelled post-hoc.
    • False-positive probabilities vary between runs; the KIC 6699887 runs span 0.15 to 3.4 %. The mean is quoted.
    • The companion prior assumes that 46 % of stars have companions, the solar-type rate, which is conservative for these smaller stars.

    Methods

    Box-least-squares search with an inverted-flux null on every star; stage-2 vetting; χ² pixel localisation per quarter and per season; TRICERATOPS (Giacalone et al. 2021); MOLUSC (Wood et al. 2021); batman and emcee transit fits. Rules and every revision are in pipeline/prereg_poolA.json.
